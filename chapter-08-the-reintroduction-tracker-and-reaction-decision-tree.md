# Chapter 8: The Reintroduction Tracker & Reaction Decision Tree

A woman I'll call Denise emailed me eleven days into her reintroduction phase, ready to quit. She'd added ghee back on a Tuesday, felt fine Tuesday, fine Wednesday, and then Thursday afternoon her hands swelled up and her joints ached like she'd run a marathon in the cold.

She was positive it was the ghee. She cut it, along with three other foods she'd tried that same week, and told me the whole protocol was "too sensitive to actually work."

Here's what she'd missed: autoimmune reactions rarely show up on the day you eat the food. Most of them land 24 to 72 hours later, which means Denise's Thursday flare could have come from Tuesday's ghee, Wednesday's black pepper, or a stressful phone call she'd had Wednesday night. Without a written record, she had no way to know — and neither did I.

That's the problem this chapter fixes. Not the food. The tracking.

By the end of this chapter you'll have two things you can start using today: a full reintroduction tracker you can copy into a notebook or spreadsheet, and a key for reading what it tells you. Together, they do the job your memory can't.

## Why Casual Reintroduction Always Lies to You

If you're reintroducing foods by memory — "I think it was the eggs" — you're not actually testing anything. You're guessing, and your guesses are shaped by whatever you happen to remember, which is usually the most recent thing you ate before you felt bad.

That's a real bias, not a character flaw. Your brain is wired to connect cause and effect that happen close together in time. It's great for learning not to touch a hot stove. It's terrible for figuring out whether coconut aminos triggered a three-day joint flare.

Autoimmune reactions break that wiring in two specific ways: timing and dose.

**Timing.** An immediate reaction — hives, stomach cramping, a headache within an hour — is rare in autoimmune disease. Far more common is a delayed immune response, where inflammatory markers build quietly for a day or two before you feel anything. By the time the flare hits, the food is old news in your memory, but it's still very much active in your body.

**Dose.** Some foods are fine at a tablespoon and a problem at a cup. If you test "dairy" by eating a full bowl of yogurt, you might react to the volume, not the food itself — and then cross off a food that would have been totally fine in a smaller amount.

This is why you need a tracker. Not because you're disorganized, but because the information you need literally cannot live in your memory. It has to live on paper.

Think about how many small decisions go into a single day of eating — what you cooked with, what you seasoned it with, what you took alongside it, how you slept, how stressed you were. Your brain isn't built to hold all of that plus a two-day delay and still spit out an accurate answer. A tracker doesn't ask your brain to do that job. It just asks you to write things down, so the pattern can show itself later.

## The Three Reaction Types You're Actually Watching For

Before you look at the tracker template, you need to know what you're looking for. There are three reaction patterns, and casual reintroduction usually only catches one of them.

**1. Immediate reactions** show up within minutes to a few hours. Think bloating, cramping, an itchy mouth, a racing heart. These are the easiest to catch because the timing is obvious — but for autoimmune conditions specifically, they're the least common pattern.

**2. Delayed reactions** show up 24 to 72 hours after the food. This is the big one for autoimmune flares: joint pain, brain fog, skin breakouts, fatigue that feels like you got hit by a truck, digestive changes that show up two days later. If you're only watching the day you eat the food, you will miss this every single time.

**3. Dose-dependent reactions** don't show up at a small serving but do show up at a larger one, or they don't show up after one exposure but do after two or three in the same week. This is why the tracker has you test a food more than once before clearing it.

Here's the analogy I use with clients: reintroduction is less like flipping a light switch and more like filling a bathtub. One splash of water doesn't overflow the tub. But splash after splash — a little seasoning here, a stressful week there, a poor night of sleep — and eventually it spills over, and it's easy to blame whatever went in last, when really it was everything that came before it too.

## The Hidden Variables That Get Blamed on the Wrong Food

Before you start blaming a food, rule out the other usual suspects. These four things derail more reintroductions than any actual food sensitivity does.

**Seasoning blends.** That "simple" taco seasoning might have anti-caking agents, sugar, or a spice you haven't tested yet. I had a client swear off beef for two weeks because "every time I eat it, I flare" — until we noticed she only reacted to the beef she seasoned with a store-bought garlic-onion blend, never the beef she salted plain. It was never the beef. Read labels or make your own blends from single spices you've already cleared.

**Supplements.** Fillers, binders, and flavoring agents in pills and powders are a common blind spot. A fish oil capsule might carry soy lecithin. A "pure" magnesium powder might carry citric acid or natural flavor from a source you haven't cleared yet. If you added a new supplement the same week as a new food, you've got two variables, not one.

**Stress spikes.** A hard conversation, a bad night of sleep, or a deadline crunch can raise cortisol and trigger inflammation on its own — no food required. Your immune system doesn't distinguish neatly between "attacked by a pathogen" and "attacked by a brutal week at work." Big life stress that week deserves a note in your tracker, not a shrug.

**Electrolyte gaps.** Low sodium, potassium, or magnesium can cause symptoms — headaches, muscle cramps, fatigue, heart palpitations — that mimic a food reaction almost perfectly. This one trips up more people than you'd think, especially in the first month of a stricter elimination phase, when your body is still adjusting to lower carbohydrate intake and shedding water and sodium faster than usual.

None of these mean the method is failing. They mean the picture is more crowded than a single food. That's exactly why the tracker below asks you to log more than just what you ate.

## One Food at a Time, With Real Gaps Between Them

Here's a rule that will save you weeks of confusion: test exactly one new food at a time, and don't introduce the next one until you've completed the full four-day watch window on the one before it.

I know the pull to speed things up. You feel good, you're excited, and it's tempting to add eggs on Monday and coconut flour on Wednesday because neither one seemed to cause trouble right away. But if a flare shows up Thursday, you're right back to guessing which of the two caused it — the exact trap this chapter is trying to get you out of.

A good rhythm looks like this: introduce the food, watch for four full days using the tracker below, reach a verdict, then wait at least one more clean day before starting the next food. Slower than you'd like, yes. But it's the difference between a list of confirmed triggers you can trust and a list of guesses you'll be second-guessing for months.

## Reading the Key: How to Interpret What You Wrote Down

Once you've got a few tracker entries filled in, here's how to read them. Think of this key the way a nurse reads a chart — not glancing at one number in isolation, but looking at the whole shape of the four days together before drawing a conclusion.

If symptoms appeared on **Day 1**, within hours of eating, that's your **immediate** category. These are usually the easiest calls — the food is probably a clear no, at least for now.

If symptoms showed up on **Day 2 or Day 3** with nothing on Day 1, that's a **delayed** reaction. This is the one that gets missed constantly, and it's exactly why the tracker makes you keep watching for three full days, not just the day you ate.

If a food was fine the first time but caused symptoms the second or third time you tested it — or was fine at a small amount but not at a bigger one — that's **dose-dependent**. These foods often aren't off the table forever. They just need a smaller, less frequent place in your diet, tested carefully over time. Think of it like a bucket with a slow leak: a little bit at a time drains out fine, but a full pour overwhelms it. Your body may handle a tablespoon of cream in your coffee just fine while a full cup in a sauce sends you into a flare two days later. That's not a contradiction — it's dose, doing exactly what dose does.

And if your symptom score never moves, congratulations — that food is one less thing standing between you and a fuller plate.

One more thing worth knowing: a single messy entry doesn't ruin the whole process. If you had a rough night of sleep or a stressful day right in the middle of your four-day window, don't throw out the data — just note it, and if the verdict looks unclear, retest that food on a calmer week before you decide anything. The tracker is forgiving like that. It's not asking for a perfect life. It's asking for an honest record of the one you actually have.

Denise, by the way, went back through her notes once she started tracking properly. It wasn't the ghee. It was black pepper, showing up reliably every time, 48 hours later, like clockwork. The ghee is back in her kitchen. The pepper is not — for now, and she plans to retest it in a smaller dose in a few months, once her gut has had more time to heal.

Once you've run a handful of foods through this tracker, you'll start to notice something clients tell me all the time: the guessing game stops. You're not lying awake wondering if it was the eggs or the stress or the seasoning. You know, because you wrote it down, watched it happen twice, and can point to the pattern in black and white. That certainty is worth more than any single food you get to add back.

That's what a tracker gives you that memory never can: the actual pattern, instead of your best guess at one.
